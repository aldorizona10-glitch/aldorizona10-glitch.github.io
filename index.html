A law student who breaks things on purpose

I study Constitutional Law at UIN Imam Bonjol Padang. I also find vulnerabilities in software, and get paid for it.

People treat that as a contradiction. It has never felt like one. Four semesters of legal training taught me a single stubborn habit: read a rule not to learn what it forbids, but to find what it forgot to mention. The article that omits a condition. The definition drawn too narrowly. The procedure that never says who verifies.

Software turns out to read exactly the same way. An API is a set of rules about who may reach what. Vulnerabilities are rarely rules that were broken — they are conditions nobody thought to check. I don't find bugs because I am a stronger programmer than the people who wrote the code. I find them because I was trained to notice the missing sentence.

Everything below is self-taught, built out of Padang, and verifiable by someone other than me.

Technical detail is withheld. The program permits me to state that the bounty exists — not to describe the weakness. That restriction outlives the payment, so this page stops here.

What that single line leaves out is the shape of the work. Most of it returns nothing. I have audited well over a hundred packages in a week and closed the notebook with zero findings, because the easy vulnerability classes died years ago in anything popular enough to matter. The paid report sits on top of a tall stack of audits that found nothing at all — and nobody posts about those, which is precisely why I mention it.

Where I spend attention now: broken access control, permission models in AI agent tooling, prompt injection and trust boundaries in LLM-driven systems. Places where a machine cannot tell you what the system was supposed to mean.
